\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
Early flood warning systems rely on timely information about water levels to alert users before critical thresholds are reached. Such systems can help to reduce the impact of floods by giving affected people and responsible organizations more time to react. Using a reconstruction of the past 500 years of European flood history from more than 100 documentary flood series, \citeauthor{bloschlCurrentEuropeanFloodrich2020} identify 1990--2016 as one of the most flood-rich periods in that record and as the only one of these periods that coincided with warmer than usual conditions \cite{bloschlCurrentEuropeanFloodrich2020}. Therefore, there is a genuine need for accurate and timely information about the development of water levels.

FloodAlert \cite{FloodAlertWaterLevels} provides a service where users can subscribe to water level alerts for specific locations. Furthermore, FloodAlert provides a web interface for viewing real-time water level data from monitoring stations around the world (available at \url{https://pegelalarm.at/paw/}). Currently, FloodAlert uses a simple threshold-based alerting system. An alert is triggered when the measured water level exceeds a predefined threshold. FloodAlert does not own or operate the measurement stations. Instead, it collects the data from different providers and provides them through a common interface. The availability of real-time and historical water level data creates the opportunity to extend the service with a short-term water level forecasting feature. Such a forecast could show the expected water level trend before a threshold is reached and therefore provide additional information to users. As a first step, the intended purpose is to display the forecast on the website, rather than to issue warnings based on forecasted values.

Data-driven methods are increasingly used for short-term river-stage and flood forecasting. One advantage of these methods is that they can learn relationships directly from historical observations. Furthermore, they have relatively low computational requirements during operation \cite{dazziFloodStageForecasting2021}. Different approaches are used in the literature, including linear models, tree-based models, support vector regression, feed-forward neural networks, recurrent neural networks, and hybrid approaches. A recent review compared machine learning applications from 94 studies. The review shows that the performance of the different model types changes depending on the forecast horizon. Hybrid approaches performed well across the different lead times, while Random Forest, artificial neural networks, \ac{LSTM}, and support vector machines performed well for specific lead-time ranges \cite{asifReviewIntercomparisonMachine2025}. This shows that there is no single model that can be expected to perform best for every river and forecast horizon. Therefore, different models have to be compared for the available data and the intended use case.

Several case studies show the potential of machine learning for water level forecasting. \citeauthor{dazziFloodStageForecasting2021} compared support vector regression, \ac{MLP}, and \ac{LSTM} models for the Parma River. The models mainly used upstream water level measurements as inputs. All three models provided accurate forecasts up to six hours ahead. However, flood peaks were predicted poorly at longer lead times \cite{dazziFloodStageForecasting2021}. \citeauthor{guoPredictionRiverStage2021} compared four machine learning models for forecasts from one to six hours ahead on a tidal river. Light Gradient Boosting achieved the best performance. The study also compared different input combinations and showed that the tidal level had a considerable effect on the prediction performance \cite{guoPredictionRiverStage2021}. \citeauthor{belyakovaForecastingWaterLevels2022} evaluated M5P, XGBoost, and \ac{MLP} models for two mountain rivers and forecast horizons between one and 20 hours. The forecast quality differed between the two rivers, even though the same model types were used \cite{belyakovaForecastingWaterLevels2022}. This indicates that results from one location cannot be directly transferred to another location.

Neural networks are often used because water level observations are sequential data. \ac{LSTM}-based models have shown good performance in river forecasting. However, a more complex network does not necessarily lead to a better forecast. \citeauthor{leComparisonDeepLearning2021} compared six neural network architectures for streamflow forecasting. All four recurrent models performed better than the feed-forward and convolutional networks, and the difference grew with the lead time. Within the recurrent models, however, the more complex stacked and bidirectional \ac{LSTM} did not improve on the simple \ac{LSTM} and \ac{GRU} \cite{leComparisonDeepLearning2021}. \ac{LSTM} has also been used for real-time rolling water level forecasts. In these forecasts the error is smallest at the start and grows with the length of the forecast period \cite{liuShortTermRealTime2021}.

Tree-based models are another common approach. \citeauthor{sandersDataDrivenFloodAlert2022} developed a flood alert prototype based on XGBoost, which continuously forecasts the water level from gauge observations. The study compared a scenario using only the rain and stage gauges inside the study watershed with a scenario that added 20 rain gauges from outside it. The aggregate error metrics were almost identical for both scenarios, but the additional rain gauges degraded the predicted arrival times of the critical stages \cite{sandersDataDrivenFloodAlert2022}. This shows that adding more input data does not automatically improve the forecast and can even harm it. Both the model and the input features therefore have to be evaluated.

The model performance is only one part of an operational forecasting system. \citeauthor{nevoFloodForecastingMachine2022} describe an operational framework that combines data validation, stage forecasting, inundation modeling, and alert distribution. \ac{LSTM} and linear models are used for stage forecasting, and the system was deployed at a large geographical scale \cite{nevoFloodForecastingMachine2022}. This shows that machine learning models can be used in an operational system, although additional components are required around the model. The incoming gauge data are validated and corrected before they reach the forecast models, because telemetry gauges regularly produce erroneous readings \cite{nevoFloodForecastingMachine2022}. Beyond what that framework covers, an operational deployment also has to monitor the forecast quality over time and ensure that a failure does not present users with misleading information.

The existing studies evaluate different algorithms, rivers, input data, and forecast horizons. Therefore, their results do not provide a directly transferable answer for FloodAlert. For the FloodAlert use case, different model types and feature subsets need to be compared with the same data and evaluation procedure. Furthermore, the average performance over the entire forecast horizon is not sufficient. The performance also needs to be evaluated for the individual lead times and for different water level regimes. This is especially important for high water levels. Such events are relevant for the users, but occur rarely in the historical data. Previous work also shows that flood peaks are difficult to predict, especially at longer lead times \cite{dazziFloodStageForecasting2021}. Therefore, a model with a low average error could still provide poor forecasts during the most relevant events.

This thesis addresses this research gap with a case study for an Austrian Danube station. A persistence baseline, a linear model, tree-based models, a feed-forward neural network, and \acp{RNN} are compared for the same 24-hour forecasting task. Additionally, different feature subsets are compared to investigate the effects of upstream water levels, weather data, and engineered features. All models are evaluated using the same \ac{CV} and a chronologically separated test set. The performance is evaluated across the complete forecast horizon, for each individual lead time, and for different water level regimes. Finally, a deployment concept shows how the forecasting approach could be integrated into FloodAlert. The contribution of this thesis is therefore not a new forecasting algorithm. Instead, it is a comparison of different models and input data under the same conditions, together with an evaluation of how the forecast could be used in practice. Although the results are specific to one station, the general approach can also be applied to similar forecasting tasks at other gauged rivers.


\section{Aim and Scope}
The aim of this thesis is to determine a suitable approach for short-term water level forecasting based on the data available to FloodAlert. The initial goal is not to provide a fully operational flood warning system based on forecasted values. Instead, the forecast should provide users with an indication of the possible water level trend during the next 24 hours. This distinction is important because a forecast shown as additional information has a different risk than an automatically issued warning. Nevertheless, the forecast should provide reliable predictions across different water level conditions. However, it is expected that model performance will decrease for extreme water levels, as such events are less frequent and therefore less represented in the available data.

The scope of this thesis is limited to the station at Korneuburg on the Danube. Focusing on one target station reduces the complexity of the forecasting task and allows for a detailed comparison of different approaches. Historical water level measurements from upstream stations and weather data are included as possible input features. The thesis covers the data engineering pipeline, the model comparison and evaluation, and a deployment concept. The forecasts are not used to trigger alerts. Results from a single station cannot be directly generalized to other stations. However, they provide a first indication of the achievable forecast performance, the required data engineering effort, and the feasibility of extending the approach to multiple stations.
