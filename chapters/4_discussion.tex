\section{Main findings}
% Shortly summarize your main findings by answering each research question in turn.
\textbf{\ac{RQ}1: Which forecasting approach provides the best predictive performance for short-term water level forecasting given the available data?}\\
XGBoost achieves the best \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} with a value of $12.43$ cm, slightly outperforming other models like \ac{RNN} or \ac{MLP}. Furthermore, XGBoost has the lowest standard deviation of \ac{RMSE} across the \ac{CV} folds. However, on the sealed test set XGBoost does not achieve the lowest \ac{RMSE}, but the \ac{MLP} model does, with an \ac{RMSE} of $12.11$ cm compared to $13.90$ cm for XGBoost. Nevertheless, XGBoost is selected as the final model, since the model selection is based on \ac{CV} performance and not on the sealed test set performance. Selecting another model based on the sealed test set would mean that the test set is used for model selection instead of only for the final evaluation. The better performance of the \ac{MLP} and \ac{RNN} indicates that these models generalized better than XGBoost to this particular test period, which includes previously unseen extreme water levels. However, based on a single test period it cannot be concluded that neural network models generally perform better than tree-based models for unseen extreme events. Therefore, based on the predefined model-selection criterion, XGBoost provides the best predictive performance, although the \ac{MLP} achieves the best performance on the sealed test set.
\\ \\
\textbf{\ac{RQ}2: How does incorporating data from neighboring stations and weather information affect forecast performance?}\\
The target station alone feature subset achieves the worst \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} across the models evaluated on different feature subsets. The addition of neighboring stations and weather information improves the \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} across these models. For XGBoost the \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} improves by about $25\%$ from $16.76$ cm (just the target station) to $12.43$ cm (all features). When just looking at either upstream stations or weather information, the upstream stations lead to a larger improvement in \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} than the weather information alone, with an improvement of $12.7\%$ compared to $6.5\%$ for XGBoost. The combination of both upstream stations and weather information leads to the best performance, indicating that both sources provide additional predictive information. The improved performance from including upstream stations indicates that their water level measurements contain relevant information about future water levels at the target station, likely because changes in water level propagate downstream. Furthermore, the additional improvement from weather information indicates that it provides information that is not captured by the upstream stations alone. Therefore, it is beneficial to include both upstream stations and weather information for short-term water level forecasting.
\\ \\
\textbf{\ac{RQ}3: How does forecast performance vary with lead time and across different water level regimes?}
With increasing lead time, the forecast error of XGBoost generally increases. The persistence model even has an advantage at short lead times of up to six hours. One reason for this is that XGBoost underpredicts the flood in September 2024, thus leading to larger errors even at shorter lead times. After six hours XGBoost has a lower \ac{RMSE} per forecast horizon compared to the persistence model, indicating that it performs better at longer lead times. When looking at the different water level regimes, XGBoost performs well for the first three quartiles, but the performance deteriorates for the highest water levels. XGBoost especially performs poorly, when looking at forecasted values where the ground truth is above the alarm threshold of 545 cm. With an \ac{ME} of $-53.08$ cm and an \ac{RMSE} of $71.44$ cm, it systematically underestimates the extreme water levels. This highlights a limitation of the model in capturing rare but critical events.
\section{Comparison with Previous Work}
% An important part of this chapter is the comparison with existing solutions and approaches found in the literature. How do the findings of the thesis relate to existing approaches? Are the results plausible? Is the proposed solution better than existing approaches?

\citeauthor{asifReviewIntercomparisonMachine2025} show that model rankings change with the forecast horizon and that Random Forest performs well between 12 and 48 hours \cite{asifReviewIntercomparisonMachine2025}. This makes strong tree-based performance at 24 hours plausible, but does not establish an advantage of XGBoost. However, the \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} of XGBoost, \ac{RNN}, and \ac{MLP} differs by less than $0.25$ cm, which is smaller than the standard deviations across the folds. Furthermore, \ac{MLP} performs best overall in the Muda River study \cite{zakariaExploringMachineLearning2023}. Thus, the better sealed-test performance of the \ac{MLP} in this thesis is also plausible, and XGBoost does not have a clear general advantage.

When comparing the absolute errors, the forecast horizon and evaluation data have to be considered. Most individual studies evaluate shorter horizons, while the operational system of \citeauthor{nevoFloodForecastingMachine2022} covers eight to 48 hours and is the closest match to this thesis \cite{dazziFloodStageForecasting2021,guoPredictionRiverStage2021,belyakovaForecastingWaterLevels2022,nevoFloodForecastingMachine2022}. However, the $12.43$ cm \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} cannot be directly compared with their results. For example, \citeauthor{dazziFloodStageForecasting2021} evaluate extracted flood events, while this thesis evaluates a continuous record. Furthermore, the rivers and performance measures differ. Therefore, the absolute errors do not show that the proposed model is better or worse than previous models.

The positive effect of upstream stations and weather data is consistent with \citeauthor{nevoFloodForecastingMachine2022}. Their models also use past and upstream stage measurements, and adding precipitation improves the \ac{LSTM} at $89\%$ of the gauges \cite{nevoFloodForecastingMachine2022}. This is similar to this thesis, where upstream stations reduce the XGBoost \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} by $12.7\%$ and weather data reduce it by $6.5\%$. The two- to seven-hour correlation lags indicate that upstream stations measure changes before they reach Korneuburg. However, additional data do not always help. \citeauthor{sandersDataDrivenFloodAlert2022} find no overall improvement from rain gauges outside the watershed, while these gauges delay the predicted peak for one event \cite{sandersDataDrivenFloodAlert2022}. Therefore, additional data sources have to be evaluated for the specific catchment.

The result that persistence performs better for the first six hours also agrees with previous work. On the Pshish test set, XGBoost performs worse than persistence at one hour, but considerably better at 15 hours \cite{belyakovaForecastingWaterLevels2022}. \citeauthor{nevoFloodForecastingMachine2022} explain strong persistence performance with the slow response of large rivers, which is also plausible for the Danube \cite{nevoFloodForecastingMachine2022}. However, the September 2024 flood also affects this comparison, because XGBoost underpredicts it at every horizon.

The poor performance at extreme water levels is also consistent with previous work. \citeauthor{dazziFloodStageForecasting2021} report increasing flood-peak errors at longer lead times \cite{dazziFloodStageForecasting2021}. \citeauthor{guoPredictionRiverStage2021} find high regime underestimation in training results at two stations, but also peak overestimation in validation \cite{guoPredictionRiverStage2021}. These findings show difficulties with high water levels, but do not establish the systematic test set underprediction observed in this thesis. However, the underprediction at Korneuburg is already visible at H+1 because the September 2024 flood exceeds the training range. The evaluation by water level regime shows that the overall XGBoost \ac{RMSE} of $13.90$ cm hides an increase to $71.44$ cm above the alarm threshold. Similarly, \citeauthor{sandersDataDrivenFloodAlert2022} report almost identical overall errors for two input scenarios, although the peak prediction for one event changes from five to 85 minutes late \cite{sandersDataDrivenFloodAlert2022}. This shows that good overall performance does not mean good performance during critical events. Therefore, the result supports displaying the forecast instead of using it for automated warnings.

Overall, the findings are plausible, but they do not show that XGBoost is generally better than previous approaches. The forecast quality depends on the river and forecast horizon, and hybrid approaches are not evaluated in this thesis \cite{asifReviewIntercomparisonMachine2025,belyakovaForecastingWaterLevels2022}. Therefore, the model and its error only apply to Korneuburg and the investigated period. The evaluation procedure, rather than the selected model, can be transferred to other stations.

The main practical impact on society and the company is the ability to provide additional information about the future water levels. However the accuracy of the forecasts are especially weak for extreme events, such as the September 2024 flood, where the model systematically underpredicts the water levels. Therefore, caution should be exercised when deciding on actions based on these forecasts, like sending out early warnings, or promoting the feature of early flood warnings, based on forecasts. Especially for water levels above the alarm threshold, the model shows a strong systematic underprediction, as also illustrated in \autoref{fig:xgboost-worst-forecast-window}. The performance of the current model is therefore not sufficient to use the forecasts as the sole basis for flood warnings or other critical decisions. Instead, the forecasts could provide additional information alongside existing measurements and warning mechanisms.

There are several limitations that need to be addressed: Firstly the limited generalizability of the model, as it has been only trained on a single target station. Furthermore the struggle with accurately predicting rare extreme events. Finally the model has not been retrained on the full training set, including instances with missing values, which could potentially improve its performance.

Only using a single target station for training results in limited generalizability, as the model has not been tested on other stations or different rivers with varying hydrological characteristics. This implies that the performance of the model may significantly vary when applied to new locations. A possible improvement would be to train and evaluate the model on multiple stations and rivers, also with different hydrological characteristics (e.g. different sizes) to get an overall better understanding of its generalizability and robustness.

Additionally water levels above the threshold are particularly challenging for the model to predict accurately, since there are relatively few instances, only about $0.75\%$ of such events in the dataset. This scarcity of events makes it difficult for the model to learn the underlying patterns, leading to systematic underprediction for extreme water levels. A possible mitigation for this could be, to train a binary classifier to predict whether there will be an extreme event, and then use a specialized model to predict the exact water level for those events.

Another limitation is the handling of missing values. While XGBoost can natively handle missing values, other models cannot. The current model comparison method requires that instances with missing values are excluded from the training and evaluation process, which may lead to a loss of valuable information. The final XGBoost model therefore does not exploit its advantage of handling missing values effectively. Furthermore this might introduce a potential bias, since the data may disproportionately represent periods where all stations have good data availability. The impact is that the model only is able to learn from periods with complete data, potentially limiting its performance and generalizability, and making real time predictions more challenging, since all input features must be available at the time of prediction. This can be addressed by retraining the model on the full training set, including instances with missing values.

Potential demographic biases, such as gender or ethnicity are not relevant for the water level sensor data used in this thesis. However, the data is biased towards normal water level conditions, since extreme water levels are rare. Since only a single target station on the Danube is evaluated, the results are biased towards the hydrological conditions at this location.

\section{Further Research}

There are many approaches for further research in this area. One direction could be to try to improve the forecast performance for extreme water levels. This could be done by developing specialized models for extreme events or different regimes, such as low, normal, and high water levels. Furthermore the inclusion of the decades of historical data (in daily resolution) could help to provide additional extreme event examples. Furthermore future research could investigate the use of more complex models such as Transformer-based architectures like \ac{TFT}.

Another potential for future research is to explore the generalizability of the model on other measurement stations. Basically applying the same forecasting pipeline to additional stations. This would help to assess whether XGBoost maintains its performance across different locations and hydrological conditions and whether the addition of upstream and weather data improves the performance consistently. Additionally including weather forecast data, not just the historical observations, could further enhance the predictive capabilities of the model.
