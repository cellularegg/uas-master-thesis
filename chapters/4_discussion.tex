\section{Main findings}

\textbf{\ac{RQ}1: Which forecasting approach provides the best predictive performance for short-term water level forecasting given the available data?}\\
XGBoost achieves the best \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} with a value of $12.43$ cm, slightly outperforming other models like \ac{RNN} or \ac{MLP}. Furthermore, XGBoost has the lowest standard deviation of \ac{RMSE} across the \ac{CV} folds. However, on the sealed test set XGBoost does not achieve the lowest \ac{RMSE}, but the \ac{MLP} model does, with an \ac{RMSE} of $12.11$ cm compared to $13.90$ cm for XGBoost. Nevertheless, XGBoost is selected as the final model, since the model selection is based on \ac{CV} performance and not on the sealed test set performance. Selecting another model based on the sealed test set would mean that the test set is used for model selection instead of only for the final evaluation. The better performance of the \ac{MLP} and \ac{RNN} indicates that these models generalized better than XGBoost to this particular test period, which includes previously unseen extreme water levels. However, based on a single test period it cannot be concluded that neural network models generally perform better than tree-based models for unseen extreme events. Therefore, based on the predefined model-selection criterion, XGBoost provides the best predictive performance, although the \ac{MLP} achieves the best performance on the sealed test set.
\\ \\
\textbf{\ac{RQ}2: How does incorporating data from neighboring stations and weather information affect forecast performance?}\\
The target station alone feature subset achieves the worst \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} across the models evaluated on different feature subsets. The addition of neighboring stations and weather information improves the \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} across these models. For XGBoost the \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} improves by about $25\%$ from $16.76$ cm (just the target station) to $12.43$ cm (all features). When just looking at either upstream stations or weather information, the upstream stations lead to a larger improvement in \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} than the weather information alone, with an improvement of $12.7\%$ compared to $6.5\%$ for XGBoost. The combination of both upstream stations and weather information leads to the best performance, indicating that both sources provide additional predictive information. The improved performance from including upstream stations indicates that their water level measurements contain relevant information about future water levels at the target station, likely because changes in water level propagate downstream. Furthermore, the additional improvement from weather information indicates that it provides information that is not captured by the upstream stations alone. Therefore, it is beneficial to include both upstream stations and weather information for short-term water level forecasting.
