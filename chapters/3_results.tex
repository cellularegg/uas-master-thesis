Values above the threshold are rare and short lived, which is to be expected. The highest water level observed is on the 15\textsuperscript{th} of September 2024, with a value of 761 cm. The highest weekly water level sum is about 274 mm, which has a $z$-score of $14.56$. The mean weekly precipitation sum is about 13 mm. As expected there is no real trend, neither upward nor downward, in the water level over time. However high precipitation and high water levels do not always coincide, as seen in on the second highest water level measured at the 4\textsuperscript{th} of June 2024 (the spike before the highest spike). There the precipitation sum is relatively low (about 37 mm). This is also somewhat expected, as the water level is not only influenced by the precipitation at the target station, but also by all upstream precipitation.

The Pearson cross-correlation between upstream stations and the target station for lags of 0--72 hours is shown in \autoref{fig:upstream-cross-correlation-heatmap}. The plot indicates how many hours later the target station's measurements are most strongly correlated with the upstream stations' measurements. A lag of e.g. 2 hours before Korneuburg means that the upstream station's measurement at time $t$ is compared with Korneuburg's measurement two hours later ($t+2$). The circles in the plot mark the highest correlation for each station, which provides an indication of the propagation delay of water level changes. The stations are sorted by the distance to the target station, with the closest station at the top. With increasing lags the correlation decreases with the lowest correlation at 72 hours being between $0.66$ and $0.75$. All stations have their highest correlation with Korneuburg at a lag of 2--7 hours. Except 207084-at, all stations also have a very high correlation of $0.95$ or higher. It is not clear why the highest correlation for 207084-at is only $0.89$. The lag associated with the maximum correlation generally increases for stations farther upstream. Station 207084-at is an exception to this pattern, reaching its maximum correlation at a lag of 7 hours, while the following two stations, both peak at a lag of 6 hours.

The heatmap in \autoref{fig:weather-future-change-spearman} shows the Spearman correlation between precipitation sums, temperature means and subsequent water level changes at the target station. The precipitation sums show a larger weak correlation with future water level changes, with the highest correlation being $0.26$. Increased precipitation over the previous 6 and 24 hours is associated with the strongest subsequent water level changes  12 or 24 hours later. The correlation pattern suggests a delayed association between recent precipitation and water level changes. The weak negative correlation for the 72-hour precipitation sum at the 48-hour horizon may indicate that the water level has already risen due to earlier rainfall and is beginning to fall again. Additionally, the long 72-hour window makes it difficult to determine when exactly the precipitation occurred.

\subsection{Model Selection and Test Performance}
\autoref{tab:sealed-test-performance-all-models} shows the performance of each model on the sealed test set. Based on the \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} the chosen model is XGBoost. The test-set performance of XGBoost is comparatively worse than its \ac{CV} performance, with an \ac{RMSE} of $13.90$. This performance decrease may partly be explained by a distribution shift between the training and test sets, as the September 2024 flood represents a previously unseen extreme event. Under these conditions, XGBoost appears to generalize less effectively to previously unseen water level ranges than the \ac{MLP} and \ac{RNN} models. The \ac{MLP} achieves a lower test-set \ac{RMSE} ($12.11$) than XGBoost ($13.90$), although the performance of the \ac{MLP} \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} is higher ($12.64$). Nevertheless, XGBoost remains the selected model because it performed best based on the predefined \ac{CV} criterion. Selecting the \ac{MLP} only because of the better test-set result would mean that the sealed test set is used for model selection instead of only for the final evaluation. Additionally, the better performance of the \ac{MLP} on the test set could also be due to the specific characteristics of this test period and does not necessarily mean that the model generally performs better. Therefore, XGBoost remains the selected model independent of the observed test-set performance.

\section{Deployment}
\subsection{Architecture}
The final step of the data science pipeline is the deployment of the model. The deployment is not implemented in the code, therefore this section describes a proposed deployment architecture through which the model could be accessed by FloodAlert. The forecasted values could be used on the website to show the trend of the water level in the next 24 hours. The model should not be used to send out early warnings, as the chosen model is not able to predict extreme water levels accurately.

The two main components of the proposed deployment architecture are Docker and FastAPI. Docker serves as a containerization platform that allows the model and its dependencies to be packaged into a single, portable unit. FastAPI is used to create a RESTful API that exposes the prediction functionality of the model to the FloodAlert service. Since FloodAlert already regularly fetches the latest water level measurements, the model can be deployed as a separate service that is called by FloodAlert on a regular basis. The general flow of a request is that FloodAlert sends a request to the Forecast API, which then fetches and caches the required water level and weather data. Before generating the features, the API validates that all required stations and weather data are available, timestamps are current and aligned, and enough historical observations exist for lag and rolling features. The full input dataset is then passed to the selected XGBoost model, which generates the 24-hour forecast and returns it to FloodAlert as a JSON response. If required input data are unavailable or fail the validation, an API request fails, preprocessing fails, or no forecast can be generated, the API returns no forecast and FloodAlert continues to display the latest observations. Alternatively a fallback model, like persistence, can be configured, thus ensuring that the Forecast API always provides a response. The proposed deployment architecture is shown in \autoref{fig:deployment-architecture}. To keep it as simple as possible for FloodAlert, the Forecast API takes the station id and the timestamp of the last available water level measurement as input parameters. After FloodAlert receives the forecasted values, it can use them to show the trend of future water levels on the website. Since the whole deployment is containerized, it can be deployed on any server or cloud platform that supports Docker.

\subsection{Model Lifecycle}
MLflow is used for experiment tracking and management in the implemented training pipeline. It allows to log and compare different model versions, hyperparameters, feature subsets and performance metrics. The deployment extends this use of MLflow with model registration and a champion/challenger process. Each registered model contains the trained model, the used feature subset, hyperparameters, training period and validation metrics. This allows to identify which model version was used for a forecast and how it was trained.

For the initial production model, the selected XGBoost configuration, including its features and hyperparameters, is used. After the sealed-test evaluation reported in this thesis is completed, the selected model (XGBoost) can be retrained on all historical labeled data available at deployment time. This retrained model is registered as the first champion model. The Forecast API loads the model currently registered as champion and saves the model version together with each generated forecast.

Once the actual observations become available, the stored forecasts are evaluated using \ac{RMSE} and \ac{MAE}. The metrics are monitored across the entire forecast horizon, as well as separately by forecast horizon and water level regime. Additionally, missing or stale input data, API failures and latency are monitored. Retraining is performed at predefined intervals or triggered if the model performance decreases over time. The training pipeline then uses the latest available labeled data and registers the newly trained model as a challenger.

The challenger is evaluated with the same \ac{CV} procedure and predefined mean \ac{RMSE} criterion used in this thesis. Its performance and configuration are compared with the \ac{CV} results of the current champion model. A human operator reviews the results and decides whether the challenger should be promoted. This ensures that model replacements are based on a predefined validation criterion and reviewed before deployment. After the promotion, the Forecast API uses the new champion model for future forecasts. If the performance decreases or problems occur, a previous model version can be registered as champion again and used by the Forecast API.
