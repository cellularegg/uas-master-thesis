\section{Exploratory Data Analysis}
This section presents the \ac{EDA}, with a particular focus on aspects relevant to the research questions.
\autoref{fig:target-water-level-history} provides an overview of the daily maximum water level over the full available period, together with weekly precipitation totals and the train/test split.
\autoref{fig:target-water-level-distribution} illustrates the distribution of water level measurements at the target station to examine the frequency and magnitude of high water events.
Data availability is examined in \autoref{fig:station-monthly-data-completeness}, which shows the availability of water level measurements across all eligible stations and the proportion of rows at the target station for which all required features are available.
To investigate the potential contribution of upstream stations, \autoref{fig:upstream-cross-correlation-heatmap} presents the Pearson correlation between upstream stations and the target station for lags of 0--72 hours.
Finally, \autoref{fig:weather-future-change-pearson} presents the Pearson correlation between precipitation sums, temperature means and subsequent water level changes at the target station, providing an indication of the relationship between weather conditions and future water level development.

As seen in \autoref{fig:target-water-level-history} there is quite some variability in the water level over time. The majority of the daily maxima stay below the alarm threshold of 545 cm. Values above the threshold are rare and short lived, which is to be expected. The highest water level observed is on the 15\textsuperscript{th} of September 2024, with a value of 761 cm. This value is in the test set which means that the model was evaluated on a previously unseen extreme event; the model was likely not able to predict this extreme value accurately. The highest weekly precipitation sum is about 274 mm, which has a $z$-score of $14.56$. The mean weekly precipitation sum is about 13 mm. As expected there is no real trend, neither upward nor downward, in the water level over time. However, high precipitation and high water levels do not always coincide, as seen in the high water level measured on the 4\textsuperscript{th} of June 2024 (the spike before the highest spike). There the precipitation sum for the week containing this peak is relatively low (about 45 mm). This is also somewhat expected, as the water level is not only influenced by the precipitation at the target station, but also by all upstream precipitation.

The alarm threshold of 545 cm is far above the third quartile; values at or above the threshold are rare, with only 765 observations ($0.77\%$ of the total) at or above the threshold.

\autoref{fig:station-monthly-data-completeness} outlines the monthly availability of water level measurements across all eligible stations, as well as the proportion of rows that are eligible for use in the model. 207340-at and 207019-at have a later start date than the other stations; they are missing all measurements before mid 2015. Station 207027-at already has measurements in August 2014, but its availability is low during the early period. Overall the availability of water level measurements is quite good, with the lowest availability being about 89\%. There are also some patterns in the missingness of the data. For example, in late 2015 and early 2016 there are some months with lower availability of the same three stations. The reason could be that there was an issue at the measurement station operator's side, or that FloodAlert was not able to fetch the data for some other reason (e.g. an outage of their system). The proportion of rows that are eligible for use in the model training is comparatively low, since it requires that all features are available. This is due to several factors, the first one being that the target must not be null for the entire 24 hour forecast horizon. A single null value in the target will make the many rows ineligible. Furthermore, all features must not be null for the row to be eligible. Nevertheless the total number of eligible rows is still quite high with about 70,000 rows.

The Pearson cross-correlation between upstream stations and the target station for lags of 0--72 hours is shown in \autoref{fig:upstream-cross-correlation-heatmap}. The plot indicates how many hours later the target station's measurements are most strongly correlated with the upstream stations' measurements. A lag of e.g. 2 hours before Korneuburg means that the upstream station's measurement at time $t$ is compared with Korneuburg's measurement two hours later ($t+2$). The circles in the plot mark the highest correlation for each station, which provides an indication of the propagation delay of water level changes. The stations are sorted by the distance to the target station, with the closest station at the top. After reaching its maximum, the correlation generally decreases with increasing lags, with the correlation at 72 hours being between $0.67$ and $0.76$. All stations have their highest correlation with Korneuburg at a lag of 2--7 hours. Except 207084-at, all stations also have a very high correlation of approximately $0.95$ or higher. It is not clear why the highest correlation for 207084-at is only $0.89$. The lag associated with the maximum correlation generally increases for stations farther upstream. Station 207084-at is an exception to this pattern, reaching its maximum correlation at a lag of 7 hours, while the following two stations both peak at a lag of 6 hours.

The heatmap in \autoref{fig:weather-future-change-pearson} shows the Pearson correlation between precipitation sums, temperature means and subsequent water level changes at the target station. There is virtually no linear correlation between the temperature and future water level changes, with the highest correlation being $0.04$. The precipitation sums show a larger weak correlation with future water level changes, with the highest correlation being $0.27$. Precipitation over the previous 6 hours has the strongest correlation with water level changes 12 hours later, while precipitation over the previous 24 hours has the strongest correlation with changes 6 hours later. The correlation pattern suggests a delayed association between recent precipitation and water level changes. The weak negative correlation for the 72-hour precipitation sum at the 48-hour horizon may indicate that the water level has already risen due to earlier rainfall and is beginning to fall again. Additionally, the long 72-hour window makes it difficult to determine when exactly the precipitation occurred.

\subsection{Test Performance}
\autoref{tab:sealed-test-performance-all-models} shows the performance of each model on the sealed test set. The test-set \ac{RMSE} of RNN is $12.96$, compared to $12.02$ in \ac{CV}. The \ac{MLP} achieves a lower test-set \ac{RMSE} ($12.11$), although its \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} is higher ($12.64$). XGBoost achieves a test-set \ac{RMSE} of $13.90$, compared to $12.43$ in \ac{CV}. This performance decrease may partly be explained by the September 2024 flood, as it represents a previously unseen extreme event. Under these conditions, e.g. XGBoost appears to generalize less effectively to previously unseen water level ranges than the \ac{MLP} and \ac{RNN} models. \ac{ARIMAX} and \ac{ARIMA} achieve a test-set \ac{RMSE} of $14.15$ and $15.99$, respectively.The flood is relevant to the evaluation because forecast errors during high water are important for the intended use of the models. The further investigation will focus on the models with the lowest test-set \ac{RMSE}, which are \ac{MLP} and Ridge.

\section{Feature Effects}
This section covers the effects of different feature subsets on the model performance. \autoref{fig:feature-subset-cross-validation-rmse} shows the \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE} for \ac{MLP}, \ac{RNN} and Ridge across the different feature subsets. All feature subsets are shown in \autoref{tab:feature-subset-definitions}. For all three models, the worst performance is achieved when only using the target station's measurements without any weather data or upstream stations. For \ac{MLP}, the best performance is achieved when using all features ($\text{\ac{RMSE}} = 12.64 \pm 2.15$); however, without the engineered features the performance is only slightly worse ($\text{\ac{RMSE}} = 12.65 \pm 1.78$). The engineered features therefore only provide a tiny improvement in performance for \ac{MLP}. This small improvement suggests that most of the information needed by \ac{MLP} is already contained in the raw measurements and the engineered features do not add much additional information. For simpler models like Ridge the engineered features provide a larger improvement in performance, with a \ac{RMSE} of $13.32 \pm 2.08$ when using all features, compared to $14.08 \pm 2.16$ without the engineered features. For \ac{RNN}, the best performance is achieved when only using the current water levels from all stations ($\text{\ac{RMSE}} = 12.02 \pm 1.96$), while using all features leads to a higher \ac{RMSE} of $13.03 \pm 2.57$. Thus using more features does not always lead to a better model performance.

\subsection{Performance Across Water Level Regimes}
\autoref{tab:performance-by-water-level-regime} shows the test set \ac{MAE}, \ac{RMSE} and \ac{ME} for \ac{MLP} and Ridge for $Q_1$--$Q_4$. The thresholds are calculated based on the training set. Therefore, the number of observations in each regime in the test set does not necessarily correspond to 25\% of the total. The unequal distribution across the regimes indicates that the water level distribution in the test set differs from the training set. The \ac{RMSE} is lowest in the first quartile, with a value of $6.55$ for \ac{MLP} and $7.38$ for Ridge. In the first three quartiles both models tend to overestimate, while predictions in the fourth quartile are underestimated on average. The first three quartiles make up about 83\% of the total scored forecasts, while the fourth quartile makes up about 17\%. Thus for the majority of scored forecasts the \ac{RMSE} in each regime is at most $11.62$ for \ac{MLP} and $11.07$ for Ridge. In the fourth quartile the \ac{RMSE} increases to $23.18$ for \ac{MLP} and $24.91$ for Ridge. \ac{MLP} has a lower \ac{RMSE} in all quartiles except the third quartile, where Ridge performs slightly better.

The number of scored forecast values in the test set with an actual level at or above the threshold is 7,414, which is about $1.72\%$ of the total scored forecasts. Thus the share of high water events is pretty low. Compared to the overall test set performance, the \ac{RMSE} and \ac{MAE} are significantly higher for the high water events, with a \ac{RMSE} of $47.33$ for \ac{MLP} and $47.92$ for Ridge, about a four times increase, and a \ac{MAE} of $32.08$ for \ac{MLP} and $30.49$ for Ridge, which is about four to five times higher. Although the overall \ac{ME} of $1.91$ for \ac{MLP} and $0.72$ for Ridge indicates a small positive bias across the complete test set, the alarm-level \ac{ME} of $-23.51$ for \ac{MLP} and $-14.09$ for Ridge shows that both models systematically underpredict high water levels on average. Furthermore the alarm level \ac{MAE} is higher than the absolute \ac{ME} for both models, which indicates that errors in this regime include both underestimations and overestimations. Ridge has a lower alarm-level \ac{MAE} and a smaller negative bias, while \ac{MLP} has a slightly lower alarm-level \ac{RMSE}.

\subsection{Model Selection}
The final model selection is based on the independent test set, while \ac{CV} is used to select the feature subsets and hyperparameters for each model family. Although \ac{RNN} achieves the lowest \ac{CV} \ac{RMSE}, \ac{MLP} performs better on the test set. Its test-set \ac{RMSE} is $12.11$ cm, compared to $12.85$ cm for Ridge and $12.96$ cm for \ac{RNN}. Thus \ac{MLP} achieves the lowest test-set \ac{RMSE} among all evaluated models. Compared to the persistence baseline, with a test-set \ac{RMSE} of $16.00$ cm, this corresponds to a decrease of about $24.3\%$. Therefore, \ac{MLP} is selected as the final model, as it provides the lowest estimate of generalization error according to the predefined \ac{RMSE} criterion.


\section{Deployment}
\subsection{Architecture}
The final step of the data science pipeline is the deployment of the model. The deployment is not implemented in the code; therefore, this section describes a proposed deployment architecture through which the model could be accessed by FloodAlert. The forecasted values could be used on the website to show the trend of the water level in the next 24 hours. The model should not be used to send out early warnings, as the chosen model is not able to predict extreme water levels accurately.

The two main components of the proposed deployment architecture are Docker and FastAPI. Docker serves as a containerization platform that allows the model and its dependencies to be packaged into a single, portable unit. FastAPI is used to create a RESTful API that exposes the prediction functionality of the model to the FloodAlert service. Since FloodAlert already regularly fetches the latest water level measurements, the model can be deployed as a separate service that is called by FloodAlert on a regular basis. The general flow of a request is that FloodAlert sends a request to the Forecast API, which then fetches and caches the required water level and weather data. Before generating the features, the API validates that all required stations and weather data are available, timestamps are current and aligned, and enough historical observations exist for lag and rolling features. The full input dataset is then passed to the selected \ac{MLP} model, which generates the 24-hour forecast and returns it to FloodAlert as a JSON response. If required input data are unavailable or fail the validation, an API request fails, preprocessing fails, or no forecast can be generated, the API returns no forecast and FloodAlert continues to display the latest observations. Alternatively a fallback model, like persistence, can be configured, thus ensuring that the Forecast API always provides a response. The proposed deployment architecture is shown in \autoref{fig:deployment-architecture}. To keep it as simple as possible for FloodAlert, the Forecast API takes the station id and the timestamp of the last available water level measurement as input parameters. After FloodAlert receives the forecasted values, it can use them to show the trend of future water levels on the website. Since the whole deployment is containerized, it can be deployed on any server or cloud platform that supports Docker.

\subsection{Model Lifecycle}
MLflow is used for experiment tracking and management in the implemented training pipeline. It allows one to log and compare different model versions, hyperparameters, feature subsets and performance metrics. The deployment extends this use of MLflow with model registration and a champion/challenger process. Each registered model contains the trained model, the used feature subset, hyperparameters, training period and validation metrics. This allows one to identify which model version was used for a forecast and how it was trained.

For the initial production model, the selected \ac{MLP} configuration, including its features and hyperparameters, is used. After the sealed-test evaluation reported in this thesis is completed, the selected model (\ac{MLP}) can be retrained on all historical labeled data available at deployment time. This retrained model is registered as the first champion model. The Forecast API loads the model currently registered as champion and saves the model version together with each generated forecast.

Once the actual observations become available, the stored forecasts are evaluated using \ac{RMSE} and \ac{MAE}. The metrics are monitored across the entire forecast horizon, as well as separately by forecast horizon and water level regime. Additionally, missing or stale input data, API failures and latency are monitored. Retraining is performed at predefined intervals or triggered if the model performance decreases over time. The training pipeline then uses the latest available labeled data and registers the newly trained model as a challenger.

The challenger is evaluated together with the current champion model on the same new test period, which is not used for training or tuning either model. Their performance is compared based on the \ac{RMSE} across the full 24-hour forecast horizon, as in the final model selection in this thesis. \ac{CV} is used to select feature subsets and hyperparameters. A human operator reviews the results and decides whether the challenger should be promoted. This ensures that model replacements are based on a predefined evaluation criterion and reviewed before deployment. After the promotion, the Forecast API uses the new champion model for future forecasts. If the performance decreases or problems occur, a previous model version can be registered as champion again and used by the Forecast API.
